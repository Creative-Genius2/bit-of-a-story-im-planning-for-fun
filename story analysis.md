# Analysis: Maurza Excerpts

## What the story is and isn't doing

There is no narrator. Not "a floating narrator" or "a narrator that follows attention" — just no mediating layer between events and prose. Third person past tense technically requires one. A narrator is implied by the grammar. The prose never lets one form because retrospection never happens anywhere in either excerpt. Every moment is still the first time it's occurring, written in past tense as if nobody has survived it yet to tell it.

Most fiction has a narrator even when it isn't obvious. The narrator is the thing that organizes, signals importance, creates transitions, assigns emotional weight. Here that function is simply absent. Which means nothing gets hierarchized. Giovanni and a Murkrow with an oversized hat receive the same quality of attention. The prose doesn't tell the reader which one matters more because there's nothing doing the telling.

## The POV shift and why it has no seam

The Murkrow shift isn't a POV shift. POV shifts require a narrative center housed in one character that then moves to another. There's no such center here to move.

The narration lives wherever the most active perception is happening. When Maurza is watching the Murkrow, it lives in her reconstructive, fragmented observation — "almost avian," "unnamed creature," "what she presumed," "to her understanding." The moment the Murkrow clocks her back — "swiftly clocked her presence out of the corner of its eye" — perception reverses direction and the narration follows it. Not by authorial decision. Because that's what the narration was already doing.

"Strange human" is where the move completes. She isn't Maurza from the Murkrow's frame. She's a category. And "just as it had always done" is a whole life that predates this scene entirely — the Murkrow's own continuity existing completely independent of this encounter.

The seam doesn't exist because the narration was never fully inside Maurza to begin with. It was always at the immediate surface of whoever was perceiving most actively. Nothing to displace. Nothing to announce.

## What Maurza and the Murkrow have in common

Neither gets their psychology stated. Both are rendered entirely through behavior.

Maurza: "That creature had to be secured; Focusing on the island's state wasn't an option." / "she had to push the thought aside with a quick mumble to herself" / "almost chuckling at its behaviour." Her personality is genuinely hard to pin down because she keeps distance even from herself, and the narration respects that distance rather than diving under it.

The Murkrow: "swiftly clocked her presence out of the corner of its eye with a sharp glance, as if it was looking down from a higher class. After cawing towards her to scare them off, it then proceeded to ignore the strange human as if it wasn't a problem... perching itself atop her head, where it began to quietly prune its small feathers with gusto, just as it had always done."

No psychology stated. Territorial, self-possessed, unbothered — all of that emerges from physical action alone. The inversion is that the animal is more legible than the human. It has no suppression mechanism. It just is what it is, completely, in every action. Maurza is opaque because she's complicated and guarded. The Murkrow is an open book precisely because it isn't.

This is also why the Murkrow works as a character rather than a set piece. Anyone can write "the Murkrow thought this was beneath it." That's characterization dropped in from outside. What happens here is the observation process itself earns the Murkrow its interiority before the narration ever enters it. By the time "strange human" arrives, the Murkrow doesn't feel like it was handed a perspective — it feels like it always had one and the narration just arrived there.

## The pacing

The pacing is inconsistent by conventional standards. It isn't controlling plot speed. It's tracking perceptual speed — how fast or slow Maurza is processing what's in front of her.

The prose is choppy at the opening because her processing is choppy. She deliberately clears her head with a mumble, and the narration immediately steadies. The pacing crawls through the extended Murkrow observation — paragraphs for a bird sitting on a roof — because she's watching carefully and reconstructing from fragments. "Until its back was turned, that is" is a single sentence. Full stop. Sprint in pacing terms. Then she hits the wall. Single beat. No elaboration.

The crawl through the observation block does something specific: it dissolves the boundary between observer and observed gradually enough that the narration's migration to the Murkrow registers as natural. The reader loses track of exactly whose perception they're in. The slow pacing isn't incidental — it's the mechanism.

## Why the two excerpts flow without a seam

Excerpt 2 ends with Maurza waking on the floor of the destroyed Mansion, injured, disoriented, barely functional. Excerpt 1 opens with Maurza disoriented on the island, injured from the same glass, barely processing what's around her.

There is no tonal cliff between them. The register never moves. Both pieces are written inside a compromised consciousness. She is functionally in a fever dream state throughout both — injured and half-blind in excerpt 1, literally dreaming in excerpt 2. The dream and the waking are the same state. That's why the prose sounds the same across both. It isn't a technical consistency. It's accuracy to where she actually is.

The glass connects them literally. It's in both. It's what injures her, what her dream kept showing her, what falls on her when she wakes. It's the one thread she can't suppress.

Maurza's suppression mechanism is the other bridge. "And yet the girl's mind shook off the thoughts as soon as it had done that" in excerpt 2. "she had to push the thought aside with a quick mumble to herself" in excerpt 1. She actively refuses to let the weight of the Giovanni encounter land, which means the reader carries it even while she doesn't. The tonal drop between the two excerpts isn't a flaw. It's Maurza.

## Past tense with no retrospection

Third person past tense implies a narrator who already knows what happened. Who survived it. The grammar creates an invisible frame — someone standing in the future, organizing events into a story. That frame is what generates the narrator in most fiction.

This prose uses past tense but embeds no foreknowledge anywhere. No retrospective distance. No sense that anyone has been through this already and is now recounting it. "Exactly what had happened here?" / "Why is it suddenly so… bright?" — those are thoughts occurring, not thoughts remembered. The epistemics are completely present even when the grammar is past.

The tense technically requires a narrator. The prose never lets one form because it never looks back.

## What the writer is doing

Writing what's physically and perceptually happening at the immediate surface, without a processing layer between the event and the sentence. The result is that events carry their own weight directly rather than being mediated through a narrator assigning it to them. Nothing is brokered.

This also describes how the writer reads. They process fiction through the experiential state of the characters — what it actually feels like to be inside the situation — rather than plot-level events. Books that had that disorienting, compromised, perceptually strange quality registered as fever dreams even when nobody else called them that, because the reader was at the level of experience while other readers were at the level of plot.

The writing and the reading operate through the same instrument.
